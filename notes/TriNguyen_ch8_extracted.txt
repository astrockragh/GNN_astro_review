## Introduction

In Chapter 7, we explored Transformers and how they process sequences using self-attention. We saw how an untrained model producing pure, random character noise could learn an astonishing amount of structure—capitalization, line breaks, and actual English vocabulary—simply by predicting the next character. Self-attention acts as a learned, data-dependent mixing operator over a sequence of tokens. But what happens when our data does not fit neatly into a sequence or a grid? 

Images live on a rigid 2D grid, which makes Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) highly effective. Time series live on a 1D line, perfect for Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs). But the universe rarely organizes itself into perfect grids. Think about a stellar stream—a tidal remnant of a dwarf galaxy or globular cluster torn apart by the Milky Way, existing as a scattered point cloud of stars in 6D phase space. As these stellar streams orbit the host galaxy, they can be perturbed by encounters with dark matter subhalos. These perturbations leave imprints on the stream's structure, such as gaps, spurs, and density variations, which can be analyzed to infer properties of the dark matter subhalos.

These structures are best represented as graphs. A graph is one of the most flexible data structures in mathematics. If we have a set of objects and a relationship between them, we have a graph. Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) are designed to work directly with graph-structured data and thus are a natural fit for tasks like node classification in citation networks. They leverage both the features of the nodes and the structure of the graph to make predictions, allowing them to capture relationships between nodes.

In this chapter, we will learn how to build neural networks that operate natively on these complex geometries. We will start by understanding the mechanics of message passing, apply it to a classic benchmark (classifying academic papers), and finally tackle a real astrophysics problem: inferring the mass and velocity of invisible dark matter subhalos by looking at the gravitational wakes they leave in stellar streams.

## The Anatomy of a Graph

Let's be rigorous about our definitions. A graph is fully described by two sets:

- Nodes (Vertices): The entities in our dataset $v \in V$ . These could be stars, galaxies, atoms, or scientific papers.

- Edges (Links): The relationships between those entities $e \in E$ . These could be gravitational interactions, chemical bonds, or citations.

Graphs can represent almost anything. A subway map is a graph where stations are nodes and the tracks are edges. The Facebook social network is a massive graph where users are nodes and friendships are edges—plot it, and we can literally trace the geographic population density of the United States just from the connectivity! In astrophysics, we often use HEALPix maps to represent the sky; each pixel is a node, and its adjacent pixels form edges, perfectly capturing the non-Euclidean spherical geometry of the celestial sphere.

### The Adjacency Matrix

While drawing circles and lines is great for human intuition, computers need matrices. We express the connections in a graph using an adjacency matrix, typically denoted as $A$ . 

If we have $N$ nodes, $A$ is an $N \times N$ matrix. If node $i$ is connected to node $j$ , then $A_{ij} = 1$ . Otherwise, it is $0$ . 

We might reasonably ask: "Are all connections symmetric?" Not necessarily. 

- Undirected graphs: If galaxy A is merging with galaxy B, galaxy B is merging with galaxy A. The adjacency matrix is perfectly symmetric.

- Directed graphs: If paper A cites paper B, paper B does not necessarily cite paper A. The adjacency matrix will be asymmetric.

Graphs can also have self-loops (a node connected to itself, placing a $1$ on the diagonal of $A$ ) and features. Just as an image has RGB channels for each pixel, a graph can have:

- Node features: Properties of the node (e.g., the 6D kinematics of a star, representing our input data $x$ ).

- Edge features / weights: Properties of the connection (e.g., the physical distance between two stars).

- Global (Macro) features: Properties of the entire graph itself, such as the total number of cycles or loops in a molecular graph.

## How Graph Neural Networks Learn

GNNs learn through a process called message passing. 

Imagine we are at an astronomy conference. We want to update our knowledge about a specific topic. We go around and ask all our immediate collaborators (our neighbors in the graph) what they know. We aggregate their knowledge, combine it with our own, and update our understanding. In the next hour (the next layer of the network), we do it again. Because our collaborators just spoke to their collaborators, we are now indirectly receiving information from two steps away.

Mathematically, the general message-passing update for a node $i$ at layer $l$ can be written as:

 $h_i^{(l+1)} = \text{UPDATE}\left( h_i^{(l)}, \text{AGGREGATE}\left( \left\{ m_{ij}^{(l)} : j \in \mathcal{N}(i) \right\} \right) \right)$ 

where $h_i^{(l)}$ is the hidden state of node $i$ , $\mathcal{N}(i)$ is the set of its neighbors, and $m_{ij}^{(l)}$ is the message sent from node $j$ to node $i$ . The $\text{AGGREGATE}$ function must be permutation-invariant (like a sum, mean, or max) because the neighbors have no inherent order. The $\text{UPDATE}$ function then combines the aggregated messages with the node's current state.

If our graph has edge features (e.g., the physical distance or bond type), these can be processed by a separate neural network and concatenated to the node features during this message passing step. The exact way we aggregate these messages defines the specific type of GNN.

### Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN)

GCN is one of the foundational architectures for graph neural networks and works by aggregating features from neighboring nodes. The message passing operation in a GCN layer can be written in matrix form as:

 $H^{(l+1)} = \sigma\left( \hat{D}^{-1/2} \hat{A} \hat{D}^{-1/2} H^{(l)} W^{(l)} \right)$ 

where:

- $H^{(l)}$ is the node feature matrix at layer $l$ (with $H^{(0)} = x$ , our input data).

- $A$ is the adjacency matrix.

- $\hat{A} = A + I$ is the adjacency matrix with added self-loops (the identity matrix $I$ ), ensuring a node's own features are included in the update.

- $\hat{D}$ is the degree matrix of $\hat{A}$ (a diagonal matrix where $\hat{D}_{ii} = \sum_j \hat{A}_{ij}$ ).

- $W^{(l)}$ is a learnable weight matrix (part of the model parameters $\theta$ ).

- $\sigma$ is a non-linear activation function (e.g., ReLU).

Why the $\hat{D}^{-1/2} \hat{A} \hat{D}^{-1/2}$ terms? This normalizes the aggregation by the number of neighbors. Without it, a highly connected node (say, a user with 1,000 friends on a social network) would completely dominate the gradient descent process simply by having a massive sum of features.

For the full list of available GNN layers, refer to the PyG NN Modules documentation.

### Graph Attention Networks (GAT)

What if not all neighbors are equally important? Just as we saw in Chapter 7 with Transformers, a GAT uses an attention mechanism to weigh the importance of neighboring nodes when aggregating information, allowing the model to focus on the most relevant particles. It dynamically decides which connections matter most for the task at hand. The message passing operation in GAT is:

 $h_i^{(l+1)} = \sigma\left( \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} \alpha_{ij} W h_j^{(l)} \right)$ 

where:

- $h_i^{(l)}$ is the feature of node $i$ at layer $l$ .

- $\mathcal{N}(i)$ is the set of neighboring nodes of node $i$ (including $i$ itself).

- $\alpha_{ij}$ is the attention coefficient between nodes $i$ and $j$ .

- $W$ is a learnable weight matrix (part of $\theta$ ).

- $\sigma$ is a non-linear activation function (e.g., ReLU).

GAT does not require explicit edge features, as the attention mechanism learns to weigh the importance of neighboring nodes based on their features alone. In fact, if we apply a GAT to a fully connected graph—where every node is connected to every other node—it is effectively equivalent to a Transformer!

The attention coefficients $\alpha_{ij}$ are computed using a softmax over the neighbors:

 $\alpha_{ij} = \text{softmax}_j \left( \text{LeakyReLU} \left( a^\top [W h_i^{(l)} \parallel W h_j^{(l)}] \right) \right)$ 

which expands to:

 $\alpha_{ij} = \frac{\exp\left(\mathrm{LeakyReLU}\left(a^\top [W h_i^{(l)} \parallel W h_j^{(l)}]\right)\right)}{\sum_{k \in \mathcal{N}(i)} \exp\left(\mathrm{LeakyReLU}\left(a^\top [W h_i^{(l)} \parallel W h_k^{(l)}]\right)\right)}$ 

where $a$ is a learnable weight vector (also part of $\theta$ ) and $\parallel$ denotes concatenation.

Pro Tip: The weight matrix $W$ for computing the attention coefficient is the same as the one used to transform the node features in the message passing step. This is a design choice in GAT. Other attention-based GNNs may use separate weight matrices for these two steps.

### The Graph Laplacian and the Spectral Domain

To truly understand what GNNs are doing mathematically, we must look at the Graph Laplacian, defined as $L = D - A$ . 

The Laplacian measures the local variation or smoothness of a signal on the graph. Mathematically, it acts as a discrete second derivative. If we have a signal that is perfectly smooth across connected nodes, multiplying it by the Laplacian yields zero. If there are sharp fluctuations, the Laplacian highlights them.

By decomposing the Laplacian into its eigenvectors and eigenvalues, we enter the spectral domain. These eigenvectors represent the fundamental modes or frequencies of the graph. Just as a CNN learns spatial frequencies on a regular grid, a GNN effectively learns to filter these spectral modes on an arbitrary, non-Euclidean structure.

### A Taxonomy of GNN Tasks

Depending on what we want to predict, GNN applications generally fall into a few categories:

- Node-level tasks: Predicting properties of individual nodes (e.g., node classification, clustering).

- Edge-level tasks: Predicting relationships, such as link prediction (will these two nodes connect?), edge classification (what type of interaction is this?), or graph reconstruction (inferring missing connections).

- Graph-level tasks: Predicting global properties of the entire system (e.g., graph regression or graph generation).

### Caveats: Over-smoothing and Over-squashing

While powerful, GNNs have specific limitations. If we make a GNN too deep, we run into over-smoothing: after many layers of message passing, the node features smear together and become indistinguishable. Because of this, GNNs are typically limited to roughly 3–5 layers. Conversely, if nodes are too far apart in the graph, we face over-squashing: the loss of long-range dependencies across many edge jumps, as too much information is compressed into fixed-size vectors.

## A First Application: Node Classification on the Cora Dataset

Let's put this into practice. Our first task is node classification. 

Imagine we have the Gaia catalog. We have billions of stars. Some of them have high-resolution radial velocities from ground-based follow-up, but most do not. Could we build a graph of these stars and use the known ones to predict the properties of the unknown ones?

We will simulate this scenario using a popular benchmark dataset for graph-based learning tasks: the Cora citation network. 

The task is transductive node classification: we train on a subset of labeled nodes and predict labels for the remaining nodes, all within the same graph. This differs from the streams example's inductive graph-level regression, where we train on multiple graphs and make predictions on new, unseen graphs.

### Building the Model

Now we build our GCN. We will use PyG's built-in GCNConv layers. Notice how similar this looks to building a standard PyTorch MLP, but our forward pass now requires both the node features x AND the edge_index to perform message passing. Unlike the streams example where we will use global pooling to get graph-level embeddings, here we directly use the final node embeddings for classification. Each node's output is a probability distribution over the 7 classes.

 [8]Python

 Copy

```
from torch_geometric.nn import GCNConv
import torch.nn.functional as F

class GCN(nn.Module):
 """
 Graph Convolutional Network for node classification.
 """
 def __init__(self, input_dim, hidden_dim, output_dim, num_layers=2, dropout=0.5):
 super().__init__()
 self.num_layers = num_layers
 self.dropout = dropout

 # GCN layers
 self.convs = nn.ModuleList()
 self.convs.append(GCNConv(input_dim, hidden_dim))
 for _ in range(num_layers - 2):
 self.convs.append(GCNConv(hidden_dim, hidden_dim))
 self.convs.append(GCNConv(hidden_dim, output_dim))

 def forward(self, data):
 x, edge_index = data.x, data.edge_index

 for conv in self.convs[:-1]:
 x = conv(x, edge_index)
 x = F.relu(x)
 x = F.dropout(x, p=self.dropout, training=self.training)

 # Final layer (no activation, no dropout)
 x = self.convs[-1](x, edge_index)

 return x
```

### Training the Model

Next, we define our training loop. Pay close attention to how the loss $\mathcal{L}$ is calculated: we pass the entire graph through the model, but we only compute the Cross-Entropy loss using the nodes where data.train_mask is True.

 [10]Python

 Copy

```
def train_node_classifier(model, data, epochs=200, lr=0.01, weight_decay=5e-4, device='cpu'):
 """
 Train the GNN for node classification.
 """
 device = torch.device(device)
 model = model.to(device)
 data = data.to(device)

 optimizer = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=lr, weight_decay=weight_decay)
 criterion = nn.CrossEntropyLoss()

 train_losses = []
 train_accs = []
 val_losses = []
 val_accs = []

 print(f"Training for {epochs} epochs...\n")

 for epoch in range(epochs):
 model.train()
 optimizer.zero_grad()

 out = model(data)
 loss = criterion(out[data.train_mask], data.y[data.train_mask])
 loss.backward()
 optimizer.step()
 train_losses.append(loss.item())

 # Compute training accuracy
 pred = out.argmax(dim=1)
 train_correct = (pred[data.train_mask] == data.y[data.train_mask]).sum()
 train_acc = train_correct / data.train_mask.sum()
 train_accs.append(train_acc.item())

 # Compute the validation accuracy at the end of each epoch
 model.eval()
 with torch.no_grad():
 out = model(data)
 pred = out.argmax(dim=1)

 val_loss = criterion(out[data.val_mask], data.y[data.val_mask])
 val_losses.append(val_loss.item())

 val_correct = (pred[data.val_mask] == data.y[data.val_mask]).sum()
 val_acc = val_correct / data.val_mask.sum()
 val_accs.append(val_acc.item())

 # Print progress periodically
 if (epoch + 1) % 20 == 0:
 print(
 f"Epoch {epoch+1:3d}/{epochs} | Train Loss: {loss.item():.4f} | "
 f"Train Acc: {train_acc:.4f} | Val Loss: {val_loss.item():.4f} | "
 f"Val Acc: {val_acc:.4f}"
 )

 print("\nTraining complete!")
 return train_losses, train_accs, val_losses, val_accs
```

## A Second Application: Inferring Dark Matter from Stellar Streams

Now we move to a real astrophysics application: inductive graph regression.

Stellar streams are point clouds in space, where the spatial relationships between particles are crucial for understanding their dynamics and interactions. GNNs are well-suited to this data structure because they can effectively model relationships and interactions between particles by:

- Treating each particle as a graph node

- Creating edges between nearby particles ( $k$ -nearest neighbors)

- Using attention mechanisms to weight particle contributions

Our task: Given the 6D phase-space coordinates $(x, y, z, v_x, v_y, v_z)$ of the stars in a perturbed stream, can we infer the mass ( $M_{sh}$ ) and the impact velocity ( $v_z$ ) of the invisible dark matter subhalo that hit it?

 streams, params_dict, train_split=[0.8, 0.1, 0.1],
 batch_size=32, k_neighbors=10
):
 """
 Prepare training data for Graph NPE model.
 Constructs graphs once during preprocessing rather than in training loop.
 """
 N_samples = len(streams)
 N_features = streams[0].shape[1]

 # Prepare parameters (log transform mass)
 params = np.vstack((np.log10(params_dict['M_sh']), params_dict['vz_impact'])).T

 # Split into train/val/test
 N_train = int(train_split[0] * N_samples)
 N_val = int(train_split[1] * N_samples)
 N_test = N_samples - N_train - N_val

 print(f'Number of training samples: {N_train}')
 print(f'Number of validation samples: {N_val}')
 print(f'Number of test samples: {N_test}')

 # Compute normalization statistics on training data
 X_train = np.concat([streams[i] for i in range(N_train)])
 X_train_flat = X_train.reshape(-1, N_features)
 X_loc = torch.tensor(X_train_flat.mean(0), dtype=torch.float32)
 X_scale = torch.tensor(X_train_flat.std(0), dtype=torch.float32)

 y_train_tensor = torch.tensor(params[:N_train], dtype=torch.float32)
 y_min, y_max = y_train_tensor.min(0)[0], y_train_tensor.max(0)[0]
 y_loc = (y_min + y_max) / 2
 y_scale = (y_max - y_min) / 2

 # Create graph transformation (set `loop=True` to include self-loops)
 transform = T.KNNGraph(k=k_neighbors, loop=True)

 # Create graphs for training data
 print("Constructing graphs for training data...")
 train_graphs = []
 val_graphs = []
 test_graphs = []

 for i in range(N_samples):
 stream, param = streams[i], params[i]

 particle_features = torch.tensor(stream, dtype=torch.float32)
 pos = particle_features[:, :3]

 # Normalize features using training statistics
 particle_features_norm = (particle_features - X_loc) / X_scale

 # Normalize parameters

 y_norm = y_norm.view(1, -1)

 data = Data(

 y=y_norm,
 pos=pos
 )

 train_graphs.append(data)

 val_graphs.append(data)

 test_graphs.append(data)

 # Create DataLoaders
 train_loader = PyGDataLoader(train_graphs, batch_size=batch_size, shuffle=True)
 val_loader = PyGDataLoader(val_graphs, batch_size=batch_size, shuffle=False)
 test_loader = PyGDataLoader(test_graphs, batch_size=batch_size, shuffle=False)

 norm_dict = {'X_loc': X_loc, 'X_scale': X_scale, 'y_loc': y_loc, 'y_scale': y_scale}

PyTorch Geometric handles batching in a very clever way. Instead of padding graphs to the same size (which wastes memory), it concatenates all the graphs in a batch into one giant, disconnected "super-graph". 

The example batch contains the following data:

- x: Node features (6D phase-space coordinates). Shape: (N_stars, 6). This is the total number of stars in the batch (i.e. all stars from all streams in the batch concatenated together).

- edge_index: Graph connectivity information. Shape: (2, N_edges).

- y: Properties of the subhalos. Shape: (N_batch, 2).

- ptr: Batch pointer indicating the start index of each stream in the batch. Shape: (N_batch + 1,). Node features of the $i$ -th stream in the batch is stored at x[ptr[i]:ptr[i+1]].

- batch: Batch vector assigning each star to its corresponding stream. Shape: (N_stars,). Node features of the $i$ -th stream in the batch can also be accessed using x[batch == i].

from torch_geometric.nn import GATConv, global_mean_pool

class GraphModel(nn.Module):
 """
 Graph model using Graph Attention Network (GAT) layers.
 """
 def __init__(self, input_dim, output_dim, hidden_size=64, num_layers=3, heads=4):
 super().__init__()
 self.input_dim = input_dim
 self.output_dim = output_dim
 self.hidden_size = hidden_size
 self.num_layers = num_layers
 self.heads = heads

 # Initial projection
 self.input_proj = nn.Linear(input_dim, hidden_size)

 # GAT layers
 self.gat_layers = nn.ModuleList()
 for i in range(num_layers):
 self.gat_layers.append(
 GATConv(hidden_size, hidden_size // heads, heads=heads, concat=True)
 )

 # Final projection to embedding
 self.output_proj = nn.Sequential(
 nn.Linear(hidden_size, hidden_size),
 nn.ReLU(),
 nn.Linear(hidden_size, output_dim)
 )

 def forward(self, batch):
 x = batch.x
 edge_index = batch.edge_index

 # Initial projection
 x = self.input_proj(x)
 x = torch.relu(x)

 # Apply GAT layers
 for gat_layer in self.gat_layers:
 x = gat_layer(x, edge_index)
 x = torch.relu(x)

 # Global pooling to get graph-level embedding
 x_pooled = global_mean_pool(x, batch.batch)

 # Final projection
 output = self.output_proj(x_pooled)

 return output
```

def train_model(model, train_loader, val_loader, epochs=50, lr=1e-3, device='cpu'):
 """
 Train the GNN given training and validation data loaders using MSE loss.
 """
 optimizer = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=lr, weight_decay=1e-4)
 device = torch.device(device)
 model.to(device)
 criterion = nn.MSELoss()

 train_losses = []
 val_losses = []
 print(f"Training for {epochs} epochs...\n")

 for epoch in range(epochs):
 model.train()
 epoch_loss = 0.0
 n_batches = 0
 for batch_data in train_loader:
 optimizer.zero_grad()
 y_true = batch_data.y.to(device)
 y_pred = model(batch_data.to(device))
 loss = criterion(y_pred, y_true)
 loss.backward()
 optimizer.step()

 epoch_loss += loss.item()
 n_batches += 1

 avg_loss = epoch_loss / n_batches
 train_losses.append(avg_loss)

 # Compute the validation loss at the end of each epoch
 model.eval()
 val_epoch_loss = 0.0
 n_val_batches = 0
 with torch.no_grad():
 for batch_data in val_loader:
 y_true = batch_data.y.to(device)
 y_pred = model(batch_data.to(device))
 val_loss = criterion(y_pred, y_true)

 val_epoch_loss += val_loss.item()
 n_val_batches += 1
 val_avg_loss = val_epoch_loss / n_val_batches
 val_losses.append(val_avg_loss)

 # Print progress periodically
 if (epoch + 1) % 10 == 0 or epoch == 0:
 print(f"Epoch {epoch+1:3d}/{epochs} | Train Loss: {avg_loss:.4f} | Val Loss: {val_avg_loss:.4f}")

 print("\nTraining complete!")
 return train_losses, val_losses

test_predictions_unnorm = test_predictions * y_scale + y_loc
test_true_unnorm = test_true * y_scale + y_loc
```

We find that the model performs well in predicting subhalo masses and impact velocities from the stellar stream data. The predictions track the true values tightly along the diagonal. 

We might wonder: can we track the gradients back to the input to see exactly which stars the model focused on, similar to how we interpret CNNs on images? Unlike CNNs, where gradients easily map back to input pixels, GNN interpretability is notoriously tricky. It is typically handled via ablation studies (e.g., manually removing the 'kink' in the stream to see how predictions change) or by directly examining the global pooled embeddings.

The model does not give uncertainties, as we only trained on MSE loss. For scientific applications, techniques such as simulation-based inference (which we will explore in Chapter 14) or Bayesian neural networks can be used to estimate uncertainties. GNNs can then be adapted as the "feature extractor" or "embedding network" in these frameworks.

As always, a reminder that as with any machine learning model, one should always stress-test the model on out-of-distribution data to ensure robustness.

## Summary

Let us recap what we have mastered in this chapter:

- Graph Fundamentals: We learned how to represent non-Euclidean data using nodes, edges, and adjacency matrices.

- Message Passing: We understand how GNNs learn by iteratively aggregating features from local neighborhoods.

- GCN vs. GAT: We saw how Graph Convolutional Networks use fixed aggregations, while Graph Attention Networks learn which edges matter most.

- Transductive vs. Inductive Learning: We successfully classified nodes within a single, static graph (Cora), and we built a model that generalizes to entirely new, unseen graphs (Stellar Streams).

- Graph Construction: We learned how to turn an unstructured point cloud into a graph using $k$ -nearest neighbors.

Graph Neural Networks are an incredibly natural fit for the physical sciences because the universe is fundamentally relational. Whether we are modeling the cosmic web, a molecule, or a constellation of satellites, GNNs allow us to bake the geometry of our problem directly into the architecture.

In the next chapter, Chapter 9 — Equivariant Neural Networks, we will take this geometric deep learning a step further. We will formalize the concepts of invariance and equivariance, generalizing the symmetry themes we have seen in CNNs and GNNs. We will explore how to build networks that inherently respect the fundamental symmetries of physics—like the Affine and Euclidean groups—ensuring that our models do not have to relearn the laws of physics from scratch.
