\subsection*{Yellow highlights}

% --- Motivation: the world is already a graph (p.2) ---
The opening salvo is a string of "everything is secretly a graph" examples --
worth mining for our own astro-flavoured version of the same move.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Graphs are ``\emph{(interconnected structures of nodes connected by
        edges)}'' -- the one-line definition we can borrow verbatim before
        swapping in astrophysical nodes and edges.
    \item The ``\emph{connectomic structure of the brain (a graph of neurons
        connected by synapses)}'' -- a vivid, non-astro exemplar of a system
        that ``\emph{[is] best reasoned about in terms of graphs}.'' Good
        template sentence; we replace the brain with merger trees / LSS.
    \item Deployment-at-scale proof point: GNNs ``\emph{serve estimated travel
        times in Google Maps}.'' Useful to pre-empt the ``is this just a toy?''
        reaction -- graphs already run in production at planetary scale.
\end{itemize}

% --- The thesis hook: Transformers are a special case of GNNs (p.2) ---
The passage I most want to keep, near-verbatim, as a framing device:
\begin{quote}
``\emph{Note that this finding does not clash with the fact that many recent
skillful ML systems are based on the Transformer architecture (Vaswani et al.,
2017) -- as we will uncover in this review, Transformers are themselves a
special case of GNNs}.''
\end{quote}
This is the rhetorical pivot that lets us claim breadth without dismissing
Transformers: not a rival regime, but a fully-connected special case. Ties
directly to our ``N regimes'' point in notes\_to\_self.

% --- Technical caveat worth flagging (p.3) ---
\begin{itemize}
    \item ``\emph{Here we assumed for simplicity that the functions f, F do not
        change the adjacency matrix, so we assume they only return graph or
        node-level outputs}.'' Marks the scope boundary of the standard
        formalism (fixed connectivity, node-/graph-level readout). A natural
        place for us to gesture at the harder, less-covered case: \emph{learned
        or evolving} graph structure.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Architectures + framing to feature in our review}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Geometric GNNs in the wild -- the hall of fame.} Lead with the
        headline successes so the reader sees graphs have already reshaped a
        science. Flagship: \textbf{AlphaFold~2} (Jumper et al., 2021) -- protein
        folding as message passing over a graph of residues, the canonical
        ``a GNN changed a field'' story. (Room to add other marquee cases as we
        gather them.)
    \item \textbf{``Transformers are the GNNs that won the hardware lottery.''}
        A powerful one-liner (nodding to Hooker's \emph{hardware lottery}): the
        Transformer is the fully-connected special case of message passing that
        happened to map perfectly onto GPU/TPU matmuls -- so it scaled, and the
        rest is history. Pairs directly with the p.2 highlight that
        \emph{Transformers are themselves a special case of GNNs}: not a rival,
        just the sibling that drew the lucky silicon.
    \item \textbf{The joke: ``GNNs Are All You Need.''} A deliberately
        ML-coded gag riffing on \emph{Attention Is All You Need} (Vaswani et
        al., 2017) -- to land somewhere with a wink (box title, section header,
        or aside). It is more than a pun: Veli\v{c}kovi\'c's own verdict below
        ("likely all we need") gives it a real technical anchor.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Passage to adapt -- expressivity / Turing-universality}

I want something very similar to this in our review (the through-line that
the simple message-passing formalism is, in a strong sense, enough):
\begin{quote}
``\emph{Do GNNs, as given by Equation~10, represent all of the valid
permutation-equivariant functions over graphs? Opinions are divided. Key
results in previous years seem to indicate that such models are fundamentally
limited in terms of problems they can solve (Xu et al., 2019; Morris et al.,
2019). However, most, if not all, of the proposals for addressing those
limitations are still expressible using the pairwise message passing formalism
of Equation~10; the main requirement is to carefully modify the graph over
which the equation is applied (Veli\v{c}kovi\'c, 2022). To supplement this
further, Loukas (2020) showed that, under proper initial features, sufficient
depth--width product (\#layers $\times$ dim $h_u$), and correct choices of
$\phi$ and $\psi$, GNNs in Equation~10 are Turing universal -- likely to be
able to simulate any computation which any computer can perform over such
inputs. All points considered, it is the author's opinion that the formalism
in this section is likely all we need to build powerful GNNs -- although, of
course, different perspectives may benefit different problems, and existence of
a powerful GNN does not mean it is easy to find using stochastic gradient
descent}.''
\end{quote}

This is also the natural home for the ``GNNs Are All You Need'' joke: pun on
top, Turing-universality + the author's ``likely all we need'' underneath.
