\abstract{Modern astrophysics will advance not through an explosion in data but in \textit{relations}, as we seldom make breakthroughs when we consider objects only in isolation. Instead we model and measure how it sits among its spatial and temporal neighbours: a galaxy in its environment, a halo in its history, a photon hit in a detector. Graphs encode exactly these relations, and Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) learn the functions that respect them. This chapter argues --- from first principles --- that GNNs suit physics and physical data better than any rival: they fit the native geometry of the domain and the signal, they incorporate the symmetries and prior knowledge we already trust and have toiled to build, and they stay flexible enough to capture what we do not yet understand. GNNs' physically intuitive workings also render them more interpretable than other architectures. We show how we can begin from the geometry of deep learning and build the graph and the graph neural network from the ground up, and how other architectures, like the convolutional network and the transformer, can be seen as special cases of one unified geometric blueprint. We survey applications across cosmology, galaxy formation, dynamics, and instrumentation; we distil a set of best practices, including when \emph{not} to reach for a graph; and we chart the frontier. Throughout, we will prioritise physical intuition and foundational principles to abstract mathematical machinery whenever possible. This follows the conviction that the knowledge of certain principles readily supplies the knowledge of certain facts.}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The history of physics is a story of geometry. Learning algorithms for the physical world must therefore be grounded in geometry.\footnote{Graph theory itself was born of a geometric puzzle: in 1736 Euler reduced the seven bridges of K\"onigsberg to nodes and edges and proved the stroll impossible. Three centuries on, the same abstraction describes the largest structures in the Universe.} From the ellipses of Kepler, through the invariances of Galileo, to the curved spacetime of Einstein, physics advances less by the collection of facts than by the discovery of the relations or symmetries that render those facts inevitable \citep{Noether1918_invariante_variationsprobleme}. A theory often earns our trust when it states the law in a form that no choice of coordinates, no relabelling, and no idle rotation can disturb.

The natural sciences run, at heart, on relations. We build theories not of the isolated object but of the relation between objects, and the \textbf{graph} holds exactly this structure: objects become nodes, relations become edges. Such data permeate the natural sciences but especially astrophysics. The cosmic web threads galaxies into filaments, the kinematics and positions of Milky Way stars trace its evolution, and the IceCube neutrino observatory scatters its sensors through a cubic kilometre of ice. None of these lives on regular Euclidean grids, and to force one upon them invents a structure neither the data nor the physics they measure ever had.

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) are fundamentally \emph{geometric}\footnote{So are \emph{all} DL architectures and this is thus not very constraining, but GNNs are more expressly geometric and are typically formulated as being a foundational part of the modern family of geometric deep learning.} in nature, and they harness relational inductive biases for functions on arbitrary domains \citep{Bronstein2021_geometricDL_book}. They propagate information along the edges through parameterised message passing, and read out node-, edge-, and graph-level quantities. The idea long predates the recent enthusiasm: recursive and recurrent precursors reach back to the late 1990s, and the message-passing view that organises this chapter can likewise be absorbed into the convolutional network and the transformer as special cases \citep{Velickovic2022_messagepassing_up}.

This chapter proceeds in three movements: the geometry of learning and the blueprint that unifies its architectures (Sect.~\ref{sec:geometry}); the construction of the graph and the graph neural network from the ground up (Sects.~\ref{sec:graphs}--\ref{sec:gnn}); and the astrophysical applications (Sect.~\ref{sec:applications}), the best practices (Sect.~\ref{sec:bestpractices}), and the frontier (Sect.~\ref{sec:frontier}).

\section{Geometry and Deep Learning}
\label{sec:geometry}

\begin{quotation}
\noindent\textit{``The knowledge of certain principles readily supplies the knowledge of certain facts.''} \\
\noindent\hfill --- Claude Adrien Helv\'etius
\end{quotation}

This section makes a promise in Helv\'etius's spirit: master a few principles, and you can always \emph{derive}, rather than memorise, the right architecture for the problem before you.\footnote{The right architecture will often turn out to be a GNN.}
The recipe takes only a few inputs, the problem you pose, the signal you aim to measure, the domains on which the data and signal live, and the symmetries to respect, and then returns the architecture to use. In the following sections, we show how to define the geometry of the data domain, that of the signal, introduce the necessary language of symmetry, and assemble the blueprint from which any modern architecture, CNN, Transformer, or GNN, all descend.

\subsection{The geometries of the domain, the signal, and the model}
\label{sec:domain-signal}

% this whole section needs to be rewritten a bit to also contain the model symmetry (the hypothesis class geometry from Bronstein)
Any learning problem hides three geometries, that of the domain on which the data exist, that of the signal we wish to infer, and that of the model. A good model respects both the geometry of the domain and the signal. The geometry of the \emph{domain} comes first: the support on which the data live, a regular grid, a sphere, an irregular point cloud, a graph. The geometry of the \emph{signal} comes second: the structure of the field defined on that support, which carries its own symmetries, a scalar temperature, a vector velocity, a tensor shear, on whatever grid we happen to sample it.

The lesson repeats throughout this chapter, so we state it once and plainly: choose the algorithm that respects the native geometry of both the domain and the signal. Impose no symmetry the data lack, and discard none they hold. A convolutional network presumes a regular grid; run it on a sphere or an irregular detector and you must first voxelise, and so invent a translational structure that does not exist, biasing the learning. Where the domain turns genuinely irregular, the graph offers not a stylistic preference but the only faithful description.

\subsection{Symmetries, invariances, and equivariances}
\label{sec:symmetries}

A \emph{symmetry} names a transformation that leaves some property untouched. The physicist meets symmetry early: the outcome of an experiment does not depend on where the bench stands, which way it faces, or when one runs it. The transformations that preserve a structure form a \emph{group}, and the way that group acts on our data, through linear maps we call \emph{representations}, dictates how a sensible model behaves. We keep the formal account, the group axioms and the representation theory, to Appendix~\ref{app:symmetries}; here two words suffice.

A function stays \emph{invariant} when the group action leaves its output unchanged, and turns \emph{equivariant} when the output transforms in step with the input. The distinction draws the line between a scalar and a vector. Rotate a molecule: its total energy does not move (invariant), yet the force on each atom rotates with it (equivariant). The same split organises every prediction we make on a graph, and we return to it in Sect.~\ref{sec:gnn}. Invariance, note, amounts to the special case of equivariance in which the output carries the trivial representation.

\subsection{The geometric blueprint, and the convolutional network it implies}
\label{sec:blueprint}

Almost every successful architecture amounts to one recipe, applied to a different domain and a different symmetry. Fix a domain $\Omega$ with symmetry group $G$ and assemble four kinds of block, after the blueprint of \citet{Bronstein2021_geometricDL_book}.

\begin{svgraybox}
\textbf{The Geometric Deep Learning blueprint.} From a domain $\Omega$ with symmetry group $G$, build:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a \textbf{$G$-equivariant layer} $B$, which mixes features in step with the symmetry, $B(g.x)=g.B(x)$;
  \item a pointwise \textbf{non-linearity} $\sigma$;
  \item an optional \textbf{local pooling (coarsening)} $P$, which shrinks the domain while it preserves the symmetry;
  \item a \textbf{global pooling} $A$, which reads out a $G$-\emph{invariant} answer, $A(g.x)=A(x)$.
\end{itemize}
Stack the equivariant blocks (with non-linearities and optional coarsening) and finish with the invariant readout:
\[
  f \;=\; A \circ \sigma_J \circ B_J \circ P_{J-1}\circ\cdots\circ
          P_1 \circ \sigma_1 \circ B_1 .
\]
\end{svgraybox}

To see the recipe at work, derive the convolutional network. Since many readers hold a firm intuition for the image and a shakier one for the graph, let the familiar case lead. Demand three things \emph{a priori} of a model that acts on an image: translational \emph{equivariance} of its internal operations, scale separation, and translational \emph{invariance} of its output. The first demand decides everything. To insist that a local linear operation commute with every shift forces it to share weights across the image, that is, to become a \emph{convolution}. Scale separation, the premise that one may coarsen the signal without loss of what matters, justifies the interleaved \emph{pooling} that stacks these convolutions into a multiscale hierarchy; a dog stays a dog across scale and small deformation. Demand finally that the \emph{output} ignore position, and a single \emph{global pooling} delivers it. Reassembled, the parts make nothing but the familiar convolutional network, and exactly the blueprint above with the group set to translation.

The same recipe, with a different group and a different notion of locality, yields the rest. The transformer becomes the blueprint on a fully connected graph, with attention as a learned, soft adjacency; the deep set becomes the blueprint on a set, the edgeless limit; the GNN becomes the blueprint on a graph, with the permutation group. We do not claim that a transformer simply \emph{is} a GNN: its positional encodings reinstate the order a permutation-equivariant layer discards, and that difference matters. We claim only that all three descend from one geometric principle. The graph earns its central place here not by fashion, but as the humble, general substrate from which the specialised successes fall out as corollaries.

\section{Graphs}
\label{sec:graphs}

Before we learn on a graph, we must say what a graph is, how we build one, and when we have no choice. This section takes the three in turn: it fixes the notation, it treats the adjacency matrix as the design choice it really represents, and it shows the astrophysical settings in which the graph turns inevitable. One point deserves emphasis at the outset: the graph picture earns its keep even when no neural network touches it. A graph path-likelihood, for one, extracts galaxy-formation information from the structure of the layered halo graph alone, with not a single learned weight in sight \citep{Yang2026_halograph_likelihood_not_gnn}.

\subsection{Definitions and notation}
\label{sec:graph-defs}

We define the graph as the triple $G=(V,E,U)$: $V=\{\mathbf{v}_i\}_{i=1:N^v}$ holds the node feature vectors; $E=\{(\mathbf{e}_k,r_k,s_k)\}$ holds the edge feature vectors, each tied to a receiver $r_k$ and a sender $s_k$; and $U$ holds the global, graph-level feature vector, after the notation of \citet{Battaglia2018_relational_GN}. The connectivity also admits a compact description through the binary adjacency matrix $A$, with $A_{ij}=1$ to mark an edge. The real graph runs sparse, so in practice one stores not the dense $N\times N$ matrix but the \emph{edge index}, the two rows of sender and receiver indices, the form on which message passing actually runs.

A graph runs directed or undirected, and the physics of the relation decides which. Directed edges suit the asymmetric, the causal, the time-ordered: a merger tree points forward in time, since to pass information backwards breaks causality. Undirected edges, a symmetric $A$, suit the symmetric relation: spatial proximity, a chemical bond, a pairwise gravitational pull. The \emph{neighbourhood} $\mathcal{N}(u)$ collects the nodes joined to $u$, and on it rests the whole notion of locality that follows.

\subsection{Building the graph: the adjacency is a choice}
\label{sec:no-adjacency}

Often the graph arrives ready-made; just as often it does not, and we must decide who connects to whom. Four standard moves answer the question. We may \emph{impose} an edge set from physical knowledge: at a neutrino observatory, connect only the hits whose separation stays consistent with a signal at or below the speed of light, a causal constraint no honest edge may violate. We may treat the data as a bare set and reach for a \emph{deep set}, the edgeless limit.\footnote{A set holds no native sense of locality: with no edges, no element lies near any other, so one cannot define a non-trivial coarsening on set structure alone. To coarsen, one must first infer a topology over the points, which precisely turns the set back into a graph.} We may assume a \emph{fully connected} graph, maximally expressive yet quadratic in cost, and so the natural home of the transformer. Or we may \emph{learn} the adjacency jointly with the task.

For the physicist the first move deserves emphasis, again and again: we can often impose the graph from what we already know. The neutrino observatory makes the clean illustration, since one setting admits all four answers, to assume the light-cone structure, to learn the wiring, to treat the hits as a set, or to connect everything, and the literature has tried each \citep{Jespersen2022_icecube_bachelor, Bukhari2023_icecube_kaggle, Sogaard2023_graphnet_neutrino, Orsoe2025_graphnet2_library, Orsoe2026_nubench_benchmark}. To learn the latent structure rewards the ambition but, absent physical priors, defeats it: the space of possible adjacencies runs vast, and the inference couples a discrete object to every downstream gradient. The adjacency matrix, in short, makes a design choice, not a fixed input.

\subsection{Astrophysical graphs: when the structure is inevitable}
\label{sec:inevitable}

Sometimes the graph offers no choice at all. Three astrophysical cases make the point, mapped onto the domain, the signal, and the geometry of Sect.~\ref{sec:domain-signal}. The \emph{domain} turns inevitable at the IceCube Neutrino Observatory, whose sensors hang in an irregular, two-layer, hexagonal array through the ice; a convolutional network cannot touch it without a faked grid \citep{Choma2018_IceCube_classification, Abbasi2022_IceCube_lowenergy}. The \emph{signal} turns inevitable in the dark-matter merger tree, whose assembly history forms a graph by construction (Fig.~\ref{fig:merger_tree}) \citep{Jespersen2022_mangrove, Chuang2024_mergertree_GNN}. The \emph{geometry} turns inevitable in the cosmic web, the point cloud of halos whose relevant structure lives in the proximity graph that threads them (Fig.~\ref{fig:cosmic_web}) \citep{Makinen2022_cosmicgraph_LSS, VillanuevaDomingo2022_cosmicgraph_clustering}. In each, the graph alone refuses to lie about the data.

\section{Graph Neural Networks}
\label{sec:gnn}

A Graph Neural Network rests on one idea, repeated: pass the message along the edge, aggregate, update the node. The rest plays variation on that theme. We open with the anatomy of a single layer, and its disguise as a force law, then catalogue the common layers, the tasks they serve, and the modules from which we assemble any of the rest.

\subsection{The anatomy of a message-passing layer}
\label{sec:message-passing}

A single message-passing layer does three things, and the physicist has done all three before. Each node gathers the messages from its neighbours, a permutation-invariant aggregator sums them, and the node updates its state from its own features and from the aggregate:
\[
  \mathbf{h}_u \;=\; \phi\!\Big(\mathbf{x}_u,\ \bigoplus_{v\in\mathcal{N}(u)}
        \psi(\mathbf{x}_u,\mathbf{x}_v,\mathbf{e}_{uv})\Big).
\]
At its simplest the layer reads $\mathbf{v}_i' = \mathbf{W}_1\mathbf{v}_i + \mathbf{W}_2\,\mathrm{mean}_{j\in\mathcal{N}(i)}\mathbf{v}_j$ \citep{Hamilton2017_GraphSAGE_inductive, Jespersen2022_mangrove}. The form looks familiar for a reason: it hides a gravitational $N$-body update. Step the velocity of body $i$ forward, and the contributions of all other masses simply superpose:
\[
  \mathbf{v}_i^{t+1} \;=\; \mathbf{v}_i^{t}
    + \Delta t \sum_{j\neq i}
      \frac{G\,m_j(\mathbf{x}_j^{t}-\mathbf{x}_i^{t})}
           {|\mathbf{x}_j^{t}-\mathbf{x}_i^{t}|^{3}} .
\]
The carry-forward term plays $\phi(\mathbf{x}_u,\cdot)$; the pairwise pull $G m_j(\mathbf{x}_j-\mathbf{x}_i)/r^3$ plays the edge message $\psi$; the sum over bodies plays the aggregator; the leading ``$+$'' plays the residual update. A GNN layer, then, amounts to a learnable, generalised force law plus a one-step integrator, which explains why message passing feels native to the physicist, and why the learned edge function so often reads back as a physical force \citep{Cranmer2019_symbolicphysics_GNN, Lemos2023_orbitalmechanics_symbolic}.

\subsection{Common propagation layers}
\label{sec:flavours}

The propagation layer comes in three canonical flavours \citep{Bronstein2021_geometricDL_book, Velickovic2022_messagepassing_up}, in order of rising expressivity and rising cost. With aggregator $\bigoplus$ and learnable maps $\psi,\phi$:
\begin{align}
\text{Convolutional:}\quad
  \mathbf{h}_u &= \phi\!\Big(\mathbf{x}_u, \textstyle\bigoplus_{v\in\mathcal{N}_u}
       c_{vu}\,\psi(\mathbf{x}_v)\Big), \\
\text{Attentional:}\quad
  \mathbf{h}_u &= \phi\!\Big(\mathbf{x}_u, \textstyle\bigoplus_{v\in\mathcal{N}_u}
       a(\mathbf{x}_u,\mathbf{x}_v)\,\psi(\mathbf{x}_v)\Big), \\
\text{Message passing:}\quad
  \mathbf{h}_u &= \phi\!\Big(\mathbf{x}_u, \textstyle\bigoplus_{v\in\mathcal{N}_u}
       \psi(\mathbf{x}_u,\mathbf{x}_v,\mathbf{e}_{uv})\Big).
\end{align}
The convolutional layer fixes the neighbour weight $c_{vu}$ (GCN); the attentional layer learns a scalar weight $a(\mathbf{x}_u,\mathbf{x}_v)$ (GAT); the message-passing layer computes a full vector message per edge (MPNN). To descend the list buys expressivity with parameters and compute (Fig.~\ref{fig:flavours}).

\subsection{Prediction types}
\label{sec:tasks}

A graph affords every task one might want, across three levels and two modes: the node, the edge, and the graph, in classification or regression (Fig.~\ref{fig:tasks}). On the cosmic web alone one may ask, at the graph level, whether the volume comes from a universe beyond $\Lambda$CDM, or what value $\Omega_m$ takes; at the node level, whether the galaxy in the halo runs quiescent, or what stellar mass it holds; at the edge level, whether two halos will merge, and in how long. The node- and edge-level outputs demand permutation \emph{equivariance}; the graph-level output demands permutation \emph{invariance}.

\subsection{Composing edge, node, and global features into one layer}
\label{sec:compose}

The three feature types, the edge, the node, and the global, need not act in isolation; a full layer updates each in turn and lets information flow between them (Fig.~\ref{fig:workflow}). One updates the edge from its endpoints, then the node from its aggregated edges, then the global feature from the whole. This order follows convention, not law, yet it composes the relational, the local, and the global into one permutation-respecting operation.

\subsection{Pooling and readout}
\label{sec:pooling}

To turn the node features into a graph-level answer, pool them. The simplest readouts sum, average, or take the maximum over the nodes, order-independent, and so permutation-invariant \citep{VillanuevaDomingo2022_cosmicgraph_clustering}. Richer, distribution-aware readouts exist: the differentiable histogram, for one, captures higher moments and stays interpretable in the language of the classical set summary \citep{Cranmer2021_histpool_deepsets}. Hierarchical pooling instead coarsens the graph in stages \citep{Ying2018_diffpool_pooling}. Read the aggregator, in the end, as any statistically meaningful summary of the neighbourhood: minimum, maximum, mean, variance, and onward.

\subsection{Scaling up: sampling}
\label{sec:sampling}

When the graph grows to millions of nodes, no one can pass messages over all of it at once. Sampling controls the cost, by node, by layer, or by subgraph, and trades exactness for tractability, the price of work at survey scale.

\subsection{The computational-modules zoo}
\label{sec:zoo}

The literature offers dozens of propagation layers, samplers, and pooling operators; PyTorch Geometric alone lists some sixty-five. Their number need not intimidate. Once one grasps the few principles behind the common layers, any of the rest sits a short step away; once more, the knowledge of certain principles supplies the knowledge of certain facts. \section{Applications in Astrophysics and Cosmology}
\label{sec:applications}

GNNs spread across astrophysics wherever the data refuse a grid. We organise the survey by subfield, and flag the honest divide between the result on real data and the result that so far lives only in simulation. The list illustrates; it does not exhaust.

\subsection{Cosmology and large-scale structure}
\label{sec:app-cosmo}
Cosmology supplies the largest graphs we have. When the likelihood turns intractable, a GNN reads the parameters straight from the catalogue: the cosmic graph over halos and galaxies constrains $\Omega_m$ and the clustering amplitude \citep{Makinen2022_cosmicgraph_LSS, VillanuevaDomingo2022_cosmicgraph_clustering, Shao2023_omegam_universalequation, Yang2023_haloclustering_graph}, and field-level likelihood-free inference reads cosmology directly from the galaxy position \citep{deSanti2023_fieldlevel_LFI}. The point cloud of dark-matter halos yields parameters \citep{Chatterjee2025_pointcloud_cosmology}, discrete tracers rise back to continuous fields \citep{Kvasiuk2024_fieldreconstruction_GNN}, and the cosmic web sorts by environment \citep{Kololgi2025_cosmicweb_classification}. Robustness across survey and simulation drives domain adaptation \citep{Roncoli2023_domainadaptation_cosmology, Lee2024_SDSS_domaingeneralization}, while topological deep learning \citep{Lee2025_topological_cosmology}, graph-based CMB recovery \citep{Adams2023_CMB_graphconv}, Bayesian lensing inference \citep{Park2023_lensing_bayesianGNN}, primordial non-Gaussianity \citep{Jung2023_QuijotePNG_massfunction}, and the hunt for cosmic voids with graph flows or the differentiable histogram \citep{Cranmer2021_histpool_deepsets, Thiele2026_void_graphflow} fill out the field. Common benchmarks now appear \citep{Huang2025_CosmoBench_benchmark}.

\subsection{Galaxy formation and the galaxy--halo connection}
\label{sec:app-galaxies}
The bond between a galaxy and its dark-matter halo runs relational, and the merger tree makes it a graph. \texttt{Mangrove} maps the merger tree onto galaxy properties orders of magnitude faster than a semi-analytic model \citep{Jespersen2022_mangrove}, and later work leaves no branch behind \citep{Chuang2024_mergertree_GNN}. The galaxy--environment connection yields to direct learning \citep{Wu2023_galaxyenvironment_GNN, Wu2024_galaxyhalo_environment, Jespersen2026_environment_assembly}, halo masses follow from the neighbourhood \citep{VillanuevaDomingo2022_halomass_GNN, Larson2024_halomass_images}, and intrinsic alignment, a vector quantity, submits to equivariant treatment \citep{Jagvaral2022_intrinsicalignment_GNN}. Clusters give up their mass from 2D observables and X-ray profiles \citep{Delgado2025_cluster_triaxiality, Iqbal2025_cluster_xray_GNN}, and generative models now emulate the assembly history and the subhalo population \citep{Roper2020_MEGA_mergergraph, Nguyen2024_FLORAH_assembly, Nguyen2025_mergertree_generative, Nguyen2026_DREAMS_diffusion}.

\subsection{Dynamics}
\label{sec:app-dynamics}
Dynamics turns the force-law analogy of Sect.~\ref{sec:message-passing} literal. The interaction network learns the equations of motion of the $N$-body system and, through symbolic regression, hands them back as a readable law \citep{Cranmer2019_symbolicphysics_GNN, Cranmer2020_symbolicregression_inductivebias, Lemos2023_orbitalmechanics_symbolic}. The same machinery predicts the stability of the compact planetary system \citep{Cranmer2021_planetarystability_BNN} and reconstructs the galaxy merger with equivariant flows \citep{Tang2022_merger_normalizingflow}; the broader programme of the learned simulator \citep{Pfaff2020_meshsim_GNN, SanchezGonzalez2020_GNS_physics} awaits its turn on astrophysics in earnest.

\subsection{Detectors and instrumentation}
\label{sec:app-instruments}
Detectors rarely sit on grids, and the neutrino telescope least of all. GNNs reconstruct the IceCube event faster and more precisely than the classical pipeline \citep{Choma2018_IceCube_classification, Abbasi2022_IceCube_lowenergy, Jespersen2022_icecube_bachelor, Soldin2025_IceCube_GCN}, an effort the \texttt{GraphNeT} library and its benchmarks now consolidate \citep{Bukhari2023_icecube_kaggle, Sogaard2023_graphnet_neutrino, Orsoe2025_graphnet2_library, Orsoe2026_nubench_benchmark} and carry to IceCube-Gen2 and IceAct \citep{Paul2025_IceAct_spectrum, VaraCarbonell2025_IceCubeGen2_ML}. Cosmic-ray observatories follow \citep{Ferriere2025_GRAND_cosmicray, Rodriguez2025_Auger_photon}, the interferometer yields to graph simulation \citep{Kannan2025_interferometer_GNN}, and the scheduling of the telescope itself, a combinatorial graph problem, submits to the same tools \citep{Cranmer2021_resourceallocation_unsupervised, Wang2022_spectroscopy_resourceallocation}.

\subsection{Stellar populations, the Milky Way, and dark-matter substructure}
\label{sec:app-stellar}
Closer to home, GNNs sort stars and probe the dark sector. Chemical tagging, the attempt to reunite stars born together, recasts as a graph problem, through attention over abundances \citep{Spina2025_chemicaltagging_GAT} and through graph kernels \citep{Ting2023_WLkernel_chemicaltagging}. Substructure in the Milky Way halo emerges from Gaia and APOGEE \citep{Berni2025_MWhalo_substructure}, the mass of the Milky Way and of Andromeda follows from the satellite population \citep{VillanuevaDomingo2023_MW_mass}, and the density profile of the dwarf galaxy, a direct handle on dark matter, gives itself up to graph networks \citep{Nguyen2023_dwarf_densityprofile, Nguyen2025_dwarf_GraphNPE}.

\section{Best Practices}
\label{sec:bestpractices}

Principles guide; practice decides. This section gathers the hard-won, sometimes deflating advice the rest of the chapter implies: which layer to try first, when to leave the graph on the shelf, how to bake in what we already know, and how to read the literature and not be fooled.

\subsection{Choosing an architecture}
\label{sec:bp-choose}
For the first attempt, the message-passing layer and the \texttt{SAGEConv} layer almost always make a sound start; the attention layer tends to train unstably, and plain graph convolution often falls short on expressivity. For the aggregator, start with a sum or a maximum, or simply concatenate several. Treat these as starting points, not commandments.

\subsection{When not to use a graph}
\label{sec:bp-when-not}
A graph suits poorly the data that already carry more structure. The regular, evenly sampled one-dimensional sequence, the image on a grid, and the very large dataset of very large graphs each find a more efficient home, though \emph{regularity}, not low dimension, makes the call: the irregular sequence of varying length sits naturally on a graph \citep{Iqbal2025_cluster_xray_GNN}. Two cautions follow. The very deep GNN risks \emph{oversmoothing}, as repeated message passing washes the node representation toward one common value; the residual connection, normalisation, or simple restraint helps. And the GNN costs dearly: the price of its generality, of the few assumptions it makes about domain or signal, runs to a heavier compute bill than a structure-exploiting model would pay.

\subsection{Embedding inductive biases}
\label{sec:bp-bias}
Do not learn what you already know. We learn faster, cheaper, and more robustly when we hand the model the structure we trust: through the symmetries we build in, and through the graph we construct, with its time resolution, its locality, its causality \citep{Jespersen2022_mangrove, Chuang2024_mergertree_GNN, Jespersen2026_environment_assembly}. None of this forbids a flexible model the same solution; it merely spares the model the trouble. The graph itself stands so strong a prior that a wrong one misleads \citep{BechlerSpeicher2023_graph_overuse}, and when a symmetry holds only approximately, a soft constraint can beat a hard one, at the cost of softer guarantees \citep{Finzi2021_RPP_softequivariance}.

\subsection{Reading the astro-GNN literature}
\label{sec:bp-reading}
A short field guide. Look for two marks: the direct line from the problem to the architecture, the geometry, and the inductive bias it invokes; and the real gain, in performance or interpretability, over the simpler baseline. Beware two red flags: the claimed invariance or equivariance the model does not hold, and the graph deployed where a grid or a sequence would serve better. In short, the graph must earn its place.

\section{Frontier}
\label{sec:frontier}

The frontier runs wherever astrophysics outgrows the point-wise statistic, and we flag a few directions. \emph{Generative} graph models remain hard, and largely unproven on astrophysical data, yet merger-tree and field generation already press the question \citep{Tang2022_merger_normalizingflow, Nguyen2024_FLORAH_assembly, Nguyen2025_mergertree_generative, Onutu2025_scorematching_cosmology}. The \emph{geometric graph}, whose nodes carry coordinates and whose symmetry therefore turns spatial as well as permutational, promises a more interpretable handle on large-scale structure, at the price of the extra equivariance one must respect \citep{GarciaSatorras2021_EGNN_equivariant, Lee2025_topological_cosmology}. The \emph{temporal} GNN, on the changing graph, fits the time-ordered data of assembly and of the transient sky. And simulation-based inference, the subject of a companion chapter, marries naturally to the GNN that learns the summary of genuinely graph-structured data \citep{Thiele2026_SBI_review}, a direction we look forward to ourselves, on real data.

\section{Summary and Outlook}
\label{sec:summary}
% DRAFT -- CKJ to review before we finalise.

We set out to teach a principle, not a catalogue. Physics advances by geometry, and the algorithms we build for it ought to do the same: fit the structure the data hold, respect the symmetries we trust, and learn only the remainder. The geometric blueprint turns that conviction into a recipe, and the GNN stands as its most general instrument, the one from which the convolutional network, the transformer, and the deep set all fall out as special cases.

Three threads run through the chapter. First, geometry: weigh the native structure of both the domain and the signal before you reach for an architecture. Second, the graph: the relational substrate that fits the merger tree, the detector, and the cosmic web where a grid never will, and whose adjacency we set from physics or learn. Third, the GNN: message passing as a learnable force law, assembled from the handful of layers and aggregators the reader can now read off and adapt at will.

The strength of the approach lies not in symmetry, prior knowledge, or flexibility alone, but in the three held together. A GNN bakes in the symmetries and the structure we trust while it stays free to fit what we do not yet understand, and it speaks, throughout, the native language of physics: the object, and the relation between objects.

The frontier runs wide and largely open: the generative model of cosmic structure, the geometric graph for the web, the temporal graph for an evolving and transient sky, and simulation-based inference on real, graph-structured data. The tools stand ready; the Universe, as ever, sits under no obligation to be simple.
